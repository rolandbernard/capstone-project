\section{Results and Discussion} \label{results}

This section of the report will present the evaluation results obtained by the developed system on the chosen test sequences. It further contains also discussion of the presented results and possible reasons for the observed deficiencies.

\subsection{Runtime Efficiency} \label{effic}

\Cref{fig:perf} shows the mean per-frame runtime of the developed tracking system across the selected test sequences for different camera count and model capacity configurations. It can be seen that across every single configuration, the detection step consumes the vast majority of the processing time. For the base setup with YOLO26n-pose and 4 camera vies, detection takes 0.0411 seconds out of a 0.0616-second total which equals approximately 67\% of the frame time. This discrepancy becomes wider when increasing the model capacity. For the larges model, YOLO26l-pose, still with only 4 cameras, the detection takes up to 0.3408 seconds out of a total of 0.3648 seconds, which represents approximately 93\% of the time.

When adding more camera views to the system using the smallest model, the computational load predictably increases. Because the detection model has to run a forward pass on every single camera feed, detection time scales almost linearly with the number of cameras, from 0.041s for 4 cameras to 0.192s for 16. Steps like matching and EKF updates also scale up as camera counts increase because these need to handle more cross-camera data points to associate and filter. However, their growth is somewhat slower than the linear increase for detection. The EKF prediction step is largely a negligible part of the overall system runtime.

When keeping the camera count at 4 but upgrading the YOLO architecture to larger capacity version, it can be observed that the cost increases significantly. Upgrading from YOLO26n-pose to YOLO26s-pose doubles the detection time, going from 0.0411s to 0.1137s. However, jumping to the medium or large version causes a massive spike, tripling the time to over a third of a second. Predictably, only the runtime for the detection step is significantly affected by increasing the model capacity and matching, EKF update, and Prediction remain almost entirely unchanged across all four model variants.

With the fastest configuration tested, the system achieves only around 16.2 fps. This is likely insufficient for real-time applications. However, it should be noted that not much effort has gone into optimization of the implementation, and that the test where performed on relatively weak integrated GPU hardware.

\subsection{Quantitative Results} \label{quant}

Quantitative results for the baseline system on the chosen test sequences can be found in \cref{fig:eval-results}. When keeping the model fixed to YOLO26n-pose and increasing the number of cameras from 4 to 16, a significant reduction in pose and localization errors can be observed. The joint position error decreases significantly from 8.250 cm down to 2.834 cm. This indicates that incorporating more viewpoints substantially improves 3D localization and pose estimation accuracy. Also, the increase in camera views brings improvements in recall. Recall increases from 0.890 with 4 cameras to 0.996 when using 16 cameras, indicating fewer missed detections likely because the cameras now cover more angles that would otherwise not be visible. Similarly, also precision increases initially when adding more camera views. However, adding even more cameras leads to a decrease in precision from 0.939 for 8 cameras to 0.934 with 16. This can be explained by the fact that more camera views introduce a higher likelihood of false positives and could also indicate limitations in the association algorithm. MOTA hits a sweet spot of 0.926 with 12 cameras for the chosen test sequences. 

In contrast, when keeping the camera count fixed at 4 and scaling up the model complexity from YOLO26n-pose to YOLO26l-pose, we see much smaller improvements when compared to adding additional cameras. The largest model achieves the best overall MOTA of 0.809 and precision of 0.915. Upgrading the model capacity also reduces MPJPE from 8.250 cm to 7.674 cm.

Next, \cref{fig:eval-by-scene} shows the average performance per scene across performed evaluations. It can be seen that the \inlinecode{160906_ian1} and \inlinecode{161029_sports1} scenes are the one with the lowest performance metrics. In these scenes, MOTA and Precision suffer from a large increase in both false positives and false negatives. For \inlinecode{160906_ian1} the reason for the poor performance is explained by the fact that it contains a small child, which is often not well visible in the cameras, especially when only four cameras are in use. \inlinecode{161029_sports1} is a scene where people are performing sports related actions which may be difficult because of quick movements. The system performs particularly well on \inlinecode{171026_pose3} and \inlinecode{170915_office1}. This is largely because in these scenes the persons are alone in the relative center of the room for long stretches of the video, avoiding a big source of errors for the system.

Finally, \cref{fig:eval-by-config} shows how different more advanced configurations of the system perform compared to the baseline evaluated above. The MOTA score increases continuously from 0.781 at the baseline to a peak of 0.897 for the fully tuned configuration, representing a 0.116 gain. The largest single jump in MOTA performance comes from the introduction of the skeletal constraints. MPJPE drops from 8.250 down to 4.917 in the tuned setup, constituting a 40.4\% overall error reduction. Here the largest improvement comes from the custom YOLO head. Both learned Kalman and custom YOLO individually provide significant performance gains, with the custom YOLO head providing slightly more performance when used individually. Combining both improves the performance further beyond their individual performance. Tuning the system thresholds yields the top-performing configuration overall, capturing a MOTA score of 0.897 and MPJPE of 4.917.

\subsection{Qualitative Results} \label{qual}

Some qualitative evaluation has also been performed for this project.

\subsubsection{Common Failure Modes}

The results of the system on the tests sequences has been visualized and a number of common issues have been identified. Some examples of these failure modes are shown in \cref{fig:qual}.

\paragraph{Partially Occluded Person}
One very common failure mode is depicted in \cref{fig:qual-occlusion}. When a person is partially occluded in all camera views, then the position of the unobserved keypoints tends to shift in unnatural ways, as can be seen with the legs keypoints in the given example. This problem is made worse by the fact that the YOLO model tend to predict invisible keypoints in unnatural positions, and while their visibility score is low, it can still commutatively have a significant effect over multiple frames. The issue causes increased joint position errors. This issue is somewhat resolved by either the learned Kalman filter and the custom YOLO head. The learned Kalman filter is able to resolve it by better modeling the behavior of unobserved states, while the custom YOLO head compensates by also giving better predictions for keypoints that are obscured or outside the image boundaries.

\paragraph{Inactive Track Seen by Only One Camera}
Another commonly observed failure is shown in \cref{fig:qual-single}. When a person first enters the room, typically only one camera can see them. This means that it is not possible to create a new track for that person. This causes false positives, and therefore reduced MOTA and Recall, whenever a person enters the room. None of the proposed configurations are able to compensate for this issue.

\paragraph{Active Track Seen by Only One Camera}
\Cref{fig:qual-single2} shows another issue that can occur when only a single camera is able to see a given person. In this case, the track has already been initialized, but the person has moved into a location where they are no longer seen by two cameras. In this case the depth coordinate from the camera can drift as the person moves, causing distorted skeletons before and behind the actual ground truth. This problem is mitigated by the introduction of skeletal constraints, because it forces the skeleton to retain approximately the same size.

\paragraph{Detection of Person While Exiting}
\Cref{fig:qual-leave} show a failure that occurs when a person leaves the room. In this case, the camera that is pointing at the exit will see the person for longer than the actual ground truth indicates. This causes false positives and therefore worse MOTA and Precision. Note that in this case we also have the above-mentioned issue of an active track seen by only one camera.
